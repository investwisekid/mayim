A liquid-cooling risk checklist for AI data centers

By John Khazraee, CEO & Founder · October 14, 2026 · 7 min read

Liquid cooling makes dense AI racks possible, and it adds new ways for things to fail. Use this checklist to find the weak points in your loops and decide what to watch.

1. Know every loop

  • Every facility water loop, technology cooling system and CDU is documented, with owners.
  • Each loop has a single diagram showing pumps, filters, valves, manifolds and quick-disconnects.
  • You know which electrical, battery and UPS rooms sit below or beside each liquid line.

2. Find the components most likely to fail

  • Quick-disconnects and fittings at the rack: the most connections, the most handling during moves and swaps.
  • Hoses and manifolds: check for wear, kinks and strain at bends.
  • Pump seals and bearings in CDUs: watch for vibration and noise changes.
  • Filters and strainers: clogging shows up as rising differential pressure.
  • Valves: partially closed or sticking valves change flow and pressure balance.

3. Monitor the signals that come first

Most liquid failures give off warning signs before any liquid escapes. Make sure you can see:

  • Pressure on supply and return for each loop, with history, not just a live value.
  • Flow per loop, compared against pump speed and IT load.
  • Pump vibration on CDUs and facility pumps.
  • Coolant chemistry: conductivity and pH, which drift with contamination and corrosion.
  • Temperature across supply and return, and thermal imaging of manifolds where possible.

4. Keep detection as the safety net

  • Leak detection cable runs under every CDU, manifold and pipe run, and around battery rooms.
  • Spot sensors sit in drip trays and at low points.
  • Alarms are tested on a schedule, not just at commissioning.
  • Automatic isolation valves are mapped to the loops they protect.

5. Turn warnings into planned work

  • Alerts are ranked by risk, so the team knows what to fix first.
  • Every alert becomes a ticket in the system your facilities team already uses.
  • Maintenance windows are planned with time for liquid-loop repairs.
  • After each repair, the loop is re-baselined so the next warning is accurate.

6. Review after every event

  • Each leak or near miss gets a short review: what failed, what signals came first and how much warning there was.
  • Findings feed back into which components you watch most closely.

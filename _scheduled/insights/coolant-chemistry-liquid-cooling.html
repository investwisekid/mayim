Coolant chemistry: the slow failure inside liquid-cooled racks

By John Khazraee, CEO & Founder · December 16, 2026 · 6 min read

A burst hose is obvious. Coolant chemistry drifting out of range isn’t, and it can quietly lead to corrosion, clogged cold plates and, eventually, leaks. The good news is that it is measurable long before it does damage.

What’s in the loop

Most direct-to-chip systems run PG25: water with about 25% propylene glycol plus corrosion inhibitors. The Open Compute Project’s 2026 guidelines for PG25 set out what healthy fluid looks like, including:

ParameterOCP guideline
Glycol concentration24.5–27.5% by volume
pH (non-OAT inhibitors)8.0–10.5
ChlorideBelow 5 ppm
SulfateBelow 10 ppm
Total hardnessBelow 25 ppm as CaCO3

Your IT and CDU vendors’ specifications come first. These numbers show the kind of limits that matter.

How chemistry goes wrong

  • Inhibitors run out. Corrosion inhibitors are used up over time. When they are gone, metal surfaces start to corrode. OCP advises restoring them only with additives the fluid supplier approves.
  • Dilution. Water left over from flushing, or top-ups with plain water, pulls glycol concentration below the target range.
  • Corrosion products. Copper, brass and steel shed particles as they corrode. OCP flags brass with more than 15% zinc as prone to losing zinc, and ASHRAE warns that stainless steel damaged by welding or grinding can release iron that fouls cold-plate channels.
  • Contamination from materials. Some sulfur-cured EPDM seals can release sulfate into the fluid over time.
  • Biological growth. Stagnant fluid and dead legs let biofilm form. OCP recommends filling with coolant within 24 hours of the final flush where possible.

The end result is the same: particles and deposits narrow the smallest passages, flow drops, chips run hotter and seals and metal wear faster.

What to measure

  • Conductivity and pH, continuously. In-line sensors show drift as it happens instead of at the next sample.
  • Glycol concentration after every top-up and on a regular schedule.
  • Lab analysis for inhibitor levels, metals, chloride, sulfate and bacteria, on the schedule your fluid supplier recommends.
  • Filter differential pressure and fluid clarity. Cloudy fluid or filters that load quickly mean something in the loop is shedding particles.
  • Every fluid change. Log top-ups, flushes and additive doses so chemistry changes can be traced to a cause.

Read chemistry with the rest of the loop

Chemistry rarely fails alone. A slow rise in conductivity, a filter loading faster than usual and a creeping rise in cold-plate differential pressure together tell a much clearer story than any one of them.

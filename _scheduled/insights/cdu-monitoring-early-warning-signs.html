What to monitor in a CDU: early warning signs of a liquid-cooling failure

By John Khazraee, CEO & Founder · November 25, 2026 · 6 min read

In a direct-to-chip system, the coolant distribution unit (CDU) moves coolant to every rack in its loop. When something starts to go wrong in that loop, the CDU’s own readings are usually the first place it shows.

What a CDU does

A CDU separates two loops. The facility water loop brings heat away from the building. The technology cooling system (TCS) loop carries coolant, often a 25% propylene glycol mix called PG25, through manifolds and cold plates at the racks. Between them sit pumps, a heat exchanger, filters, an expansion tank and the sensors that control it all.

The signals that come first

SignalWhereWhat a change can mean
Pump discharge pressureAfter each pumpA spike suggests a blockage; a sudden drop suggests cavitation or a leak
Differential pressureAcross filters, cold plates and the heat exchangerA slow rise means fouling, filter loading or growing flow resistance
FlowTCS supply and branch linesLess coolant reaching racks than the pumps should deliver
Supply and return temperatureBoth loops, heat exchanger in and outLess heat removed for the same load, often a sign of heat exchanger fouling
Pump vibration and currentPump motors and bearingsBearing wear, cavitation or a pump working harder to hold flow
Expansion tank level and top-upsReservoir or expansion tankA loop that keeps needing fluid is losing it somewhere
Conductivity and pHTCS loopCoolant chemistry drifting toward corrosion or contamination

Watch for air

ASHRAE’s TC 9.9 bulletin on direct-to-chip loops warns that entrained air reduces heat transfer, makes pumps cavitate and speeds up corrosion, and that it can appear weeks after startup. In the data, air often looks like noisy pump pressure and rising pump vibration at a steady load.

Filters tell you about the whole loop

The Open Compute Project’s PG25 guidelines note that some CDUs use 25 or 50 micron inline filters and recommend side-stream filtration on larger systems. Filter differential pressure is one of the most useful trends you have: a filter that loads faster than usual means something in the loop is shedding particles, such as corrosion products or debris left from installation.

Read the signals together

Single readings are easy to misread. Combinations are much clearer:

  • Falling pressure, falling tank level, steady load: fluid is leaving the loop.
  • Rising filter or cold-plate differential pressure plus a wider temperature gap: fouling is building up.
  • Pump current up, flow flat: the pump is fighting a restriction.
  • Noisy pressure plus rising vibration: air or cavitation.

Thresholds vs. trends

Most CDUs ship with high and low alarms. Those are needed, but they fire late: a filter can load for weeks before it trips a limit. Trending each signal against that loop’s own normal, at the same load and season, shows the drift while there is still time to plan a repair.

A short routine

  • Log CDU pressures, flows, temperatures and tank level continuously, not only alarms.
  • Record every top-up, filter change and maintenance action so changes in the data can be explained.
  • Re-baseline each loop after hardware moves, filter changes and coolant work.
  • Review the trends weekly, starting with the loops that serve your densest racks.
